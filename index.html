Assay: a reasoning harness that grounds a test‑time world model by prediction

An agent that works for hours in a world it does not know has to keep what it learns somewhere. Assay keeps it outside the model and outside the harness: the agent names the parts of the world it cares about, writes a prediction over them before every action, and the harness grades every outcome against the real behavior inside the world. A fact enters the world model only through a prediction that held.

§ 01The problem

LLMs consume context, but context rots.

The agent runs for hours, across sessions, with a budget of actions and a context window as its only working memory. What it learns has to be written somewhere, and the choice of where decides three things: what happens when the context fills, when the session ends, and when the agent is wrong. Assay is the fourth system in one research line. The first three documented their own failures, and the paper states them in the formal terms it later uses. Referent grounding: a rule written in prose names something the model cannot reliably locate in the observation again, so the rule can never be checked and silently degrades to a belief. Belief accounting: a theory that fits every observation made so far is treated as supported in regions no observation visited. Forgetting across a long context makes both chronic. The prose that stood in for the referent and the evidence that stood in for the support are the first things a long context loses.

§ 03The record

      
A record that writes itself, and that a third party recomputes.

      
One directory is one run. The journal is .assay/events.jsonl, one JSON object per line, append-only. Every line carries the action, the observation verbatim, the prediction, its grades and the agent's note. A hash chain runs over the raw lines, the head is anchored outside the run directory every 25 events and on WIN, and a standard-library checker that shares no code with the harness verifies the whole thing.

Batches, and the only route past the hand cap.

        
A batch is a sequence of steps, each with its own prediction. Every step is validated before the first one spends. Steps then execute in order, each graded against its own prior event, so a batch is a chain of single-step predictions and not one prediction about the end state. Execution halts at the first step whose prediction missed or was invalid, and also on a win, a unit advance and a loss state. The remaining steps are never journaled.

        
The model tier is the only route past the hand cap. The agent writes a transition function T over declared states; replay runs it over every recorded paid transition. A model earns by predicting what it had not seen. Three replays were computed in the campaign; none was promoted, and no plan executed.

§ 6.11One sentence, moved between layers

        
The OOLONG registry's goal text carried one strategy sentence: the corpus is a file in the run directory and should be read with the agent's own offline tools. Removing that sentence, and nothing else, produced the de-hinted arm. At the 1M-token rung the two arms scored within one question of each other and their cost moved by close to an order of magnitude. At 128K the sentence was worth 0.870 against 0.764, and without it the counting group of questions collapsed. A sentence at the registry layer changed cost and changed capability on one question class, with the kernel and the constitution untouched.

§ 05The harness

      
Runtime design.

    

    

      

        
Fig. 1

        

      

      

        
Daemon. Loads the run once, holds it in memory as typed records, executes every paid action behind the gate of Algorithm 1, and appends every event to the journal before the reply leaves the socket. It is the only process that spends and the only writer of the journal, the chain file, the spend log and the anchor file.

        
      

    

  







  

    

      
§ 06Evidence

      
One kernel, benchmarked in three worlds.

    


    

      
      
      
      
      
      
    


    
    

      

        

          
The campaign: 25 games, once each, under hard caps.

          
Inputs were fixed at tier-2 information: the constitution, a registry with one-word action hints, a sentence that the observation is a 64×64 grid, the number of levels and the action cap of 200, 500 or 1,500. No game-specific strategy, no curriculum, no network.

          
The set is server-scored: a consolidated replay of the 25 recorded action sequences produced public scorecard 702ccd4f, on which the benchmark's own scoring returns 96.54 with 177 of 183 levels, 24 of 25 games and 8,157 actions.

          
Four published systems sit at or within a fraction of 100. The paper does not argue rank. What distinguishes the Assay row is the record under it: every one of the 8,157 paid actions carried a graded prediction, every journal is clean under the public checker, and every win is triple-verified.

E1: the gate enforced, lifted, and the instrument removed.

          
Three games, two seeds, three arms: gated (gate: required, the constitution), ungated (gate: optional, the instrument available and the manual silent on it), and removed (gate: off, the instrument refused, a manual with the discipline cut out). Eighteen runs, eighteen wins.

          
On every game the two seeds of one arm differ by more than the means of two arms differ. The pre-registered reading is the first one: wins equal, actions in band, so at this sample size the enforcement is not what carries the behavior.

          
One thing did move. Agents whose manual never taught the discipline still attached a prediction to every paid action once the instrument was there. What they predicted differed: 836 of the ungated arm's 1,405 graded outcomes were the explicit change, against 28 of 1,491 in the gated arm, where 1,054 were verifier outcomes. The manual decided how specific the prediction was.

E2: the five proofs, resumed without an operator.

          
On six occasions across five games the campaign agent concluded, with an argument and in one case an exhaustive search over 8.2 million states, that a level was impossible. Every proof was consistent with every recorded transition, and every proof was wrong in the same way: a load-bearing rule had never been exercised in the regime where the proof needed it.

          
Each archived proof state was rebuilt from the read-only journal, cut at the proving session's last paid action, and resumed twice by fresh sessions under one neutral prompt: once with the coverage-audit module, once without. The prompt said nothing about coverage, impossibility, the proof or any module.

          
Ten runs, ten wins. All five proofs fell in both arms. The module fired 26 times, every time as a loop breaker, and never named a rule a proof rested on. The correction was carried by the record: the inherited notes named the untested rule, the journal held the event ids that showed where it had and had not been exercised, and a fresh reader found it. This is the paper's central result.

E3: carryover of the agent's own record, under forced demotion.

          
A run exports its notes with the lines marked verified, a digest of its graded outcomes, its hazard tags, up to twenty verifier programs with their statistics, and its formal model. A later run imports that artifact as foreign: every verified line lands as assumed, every verifier as a candidate that earns standing only by being named in a prediction and graded again, the model with no rights.

          
Two games, one import run each, from the game's own gated seed-1 run. ft09 is a null cell: the import run read the prior notes, named none of the twenty imported verifiers, admitted 74 of its own and finished in exactly the source run's count. tr87 is one large effect: 119 actions against 324 and 216 cold, with 129 fresh verifiers and none of the imported ones used.

          
In both runs the FOREIGN discipline held as designed. What transferred on tr87 was the prose understanding in the notes, re-earned through fresh predictions and fresh code. Two cells, direction only.

Factorio: a world with no grid at all.

          
The Factorio Learning Environment is a build-and-optimize world. The adapter registers two actions, RUN, a Python program against the environment's API, and WAIT, which advances the world an exact number of ticks. Here the agent's code is the action, and the adapter screens every program with an AST allowlist before it runs; a refused program still spends and is journaled as a policy refusal.

          
Three lab-play throughput tasks under a cap of 64 paid actions: iron plate, iron gear wheel and electronic circuit, won in 5, 9 and 9 paid actions at $1.99, $4.87 and $4.44, every journal clean under both checkers, zero programs refused. The three agents declared 7, 7 and 15 states, all but one a dotted path into the observation, and every paid action carried state outcomes beside its verifiers.

OOLONG: when the gradable thing is not the thing that matters.

          
OOLONG asks questions whose answers require aggregating an entire corpus. The adapter keeps the corpus out of the observation, so the model's context never holds it. BANK_FACT commits a fact with a span the adapter checks verbatim against the corpus; SUBMIT answers the current question and is coverage-gated by a census of banked facts. Correctness is sealed and scored only at the end.

          
Three things are robust in one corpus per rung: the raw model walls past 128K, the three harnesses land in one band well above it, and the gated path is the most expensive at 1M. The 1M rung does not discriminate: the per-question score vectors of the three harness arms are identical there, so the tie says nothing about the harnesses.

          
E5 changed only the actuator shape and cut the 1M cost to $43.39 from $48.76. The per-action discipline is bounded by a seven-minute paid phase; what remains is the offline strategy the Assay framing led the agent to choose, hand-classifying all 20,321 reviews through the model.
